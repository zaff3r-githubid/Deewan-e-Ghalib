10:T613,This opening couplet sets a melancholic and reflective tone. Ghalib, through the persona of the lover, laments a profound change within himself. The "arz-e-niyaz-e-ishq" refers to the humble petition or expression of devotion that a lover typically offers to the beloved. The poet feels that his heart has lost the capacity, the purity, or perhaps the intensity required for such a sacred act. It's a confession of spiritual bankruptcy in the realm of love.

The second line deepens this self-reproach. The "dil pe naaz tha" signifies a past state of pride, where the heart was perhaps robust, resilient, full of passion, or capable of profound feeling. This pride could stem from its capacity to love deeply, to endure pain, or to remain steadfast. The crushing realization "woh dil nahin raha" indicates a complete transformation or loss. It suggests that the trials of love, the relentless indifference of the beloved, or perhaps the sheer passage of time and disillusionment, have withered that once-proud heart, rendering it incapable of its former grandeur and devotion.

This couplet can be interpreted on multiple levels. On one hand, it's a personal lament of a lover whose spirit has been broken. On another, it could hint at a broader philosophical disillusionment, where the very essence of human passion and its ability to connect with the divine (often symbolized by the beloved) has been compromised. The loss of the "proud heart" is not just about emotional capacity, but perhaps a loss of self-identity and purpose in the context of love.11:T6ba,This couplet marks a shift from personal lament to a more assertive, almost dismissive stance towards trivial human preoccupations. Ghalib addresses an implied listener, perhaps someone caught in the endless philosophical or emotional tug-of-war between happiness and sorrow ("behs-e-gham-o-shaadi"). He labels such a person "muflis" (impoverished), suggesting that to be consumed by this binary is to lack true insight or spiritual wealth. The poet implies that such debates are ultimately fruitless and distract from deeper truths.

The second line declares the poet's detachment from this fruitless pursuit. He asserts that he is no longer "shamil" (included) among the "be-hasilaan-e-dahr" (aimless/unproductive ones of the world). This signifies a profound intellectual and spiritual evolution. Having perhaps experienced the depths of both joy and sorrow, and found them ultimately transient or superficial, the poet has transcended their duality. He has moved beyond the mere 'having' or 'not having' of emotional states to a more philosophical understanding, perhaps implying a state of resignation or profound wisdom that sees beyond the immediate ebb and flow of worldly concerns.

This couplet showcases Ghalib's philosophical depth, where he often critiques conventional understandings of life and emotion. It's a declaration of intellectual independence, a refusal to partake in what he considers trivial discourse. The 'impoverished' state isn't financial, but intellectual and spiritual, highlighting a mind trapped in superficial considerations rather than grappling with the essence of existence. The poet positions himself as someone who has seen through the illusions of worldly attachments and debates.12:T751,This couplet uses complex imagery of hunting and sacrifice to express a profound sense of loss and despair. "Said-e-tapidan" refers to a prey that is still throbbing after being struck, indicating it's fresh and full of life, yet captured. The phrase "murdum shumar-e-said-e-tapidan" refers to those who count such vibrant, pulsating prey. Ghalib questions their existence, implying that even the capacity to find something lively or worthy of capturing, even in its dying throes, has vanished from the world, or at least from his perception. This speaks to a general desolation, a world where even vital, suffering beauty is no longer registered or appreciated.

The second line elaborates on this desolation with a striking metaphor. "Teer-e-nigah" is the 'arrow of glance' – a classic romantic image where the beloved's captivating gaze pierces the lover's heart. Ghalib states that not only is the powerful, striking 'arrow of glance' ineffective or absent, but even the "par-e-bismil" (the wing of a sacrificed bird) is no more. A 'bismil' is an animal ritually slaughtered, often still flapping its wings in its dying moments. The wing, here, symbolizes the last vestiges of life, resistance, or even attraction. The absence of "par-e-bismil" suggests an utter exhaustion, a complete lack of life force or capacity for response, even in death.

This couplet paints a picture of extreme spiritual and emotional barrenness. The world has become so inert or the poet so detached that the vibrancy of life and even the dignity or pathos of death (the throbbing prey, the flapping wing) are no longer observable or affecting. It's a lament not just for personal loss, but for a general decay in the sensitivity and vitality of existence itself. The metaphors evoke a powerful image of a world drained of its essence, where even the most poignant spectacles fail to stir.13:T7bd,This couplet is a direct address, almost an exclamation, to the deceptive nature of existence. "Fareb-e-alam-e-faani" refers to the illusion or trickery inherent in the fleeting, mortal world. Ghalib acknowledges this deceit, suggesting a realization that worldly attachments and perceptions are ultimately misleading. The subsequent phrase, "Khuda fareb" (divine deception), elevates this idea to a metaphysical level, implying that even the grand design or reality orchestrated by God can appear as a form of deception from a human perspective, perhaps due to its inscrutability or the illusions it casts. This isn't necessarily a blasphemous statement but rather an expression of profound philosophical bewilderment and a recognition of the limits of human understanding in the face of divine mystery.

The second line brings this philosophical realization back to the poet's personal state. "Ab koi mere haal mein kamil nahin raha" means "now no one remains perfect in my condition." This can be interpreted in several ways. It might mean that among those who share his condition (his suffering, his disillusionment), none have achieved a state of perfection or complete understanding. Or, more profoundly, it could mean that within his own state of being, the possibility of "perfection" – be it perfect love, perfect understanding, or perfect faith – has vanished. The constant experience of deceit, both worldly and divine, has rendered any aspiration for a 'perfect' state impossible or irrelevant.

This couplet showcases Ghalib's characteristic blend of introspection and critique of existential realities. He grapples with the grand questions of illusion and reality, of human perception versus divine truth, ultimately concluding with a personal admission of a lost capacity for 'perfection' or a loss of belief in its attainment within his current state. The 'deception' isn't just external; it has permeated his very being, altering his capacity for ideal states.14:T73c,This couplet expresses a profound internal conflict and a sense of inadequacy. The first line, "Main kis tarah kahoon ke gumaan hai wajood par" (How can I say that I have doubt about existence), implies a reluctance or inability to articulate a fundamental skepticism about existence itself. The poet is grappling with existential doubts, perhaps questioning the reality or purpose of his own being, or existence in general. However, he finds himself unable to voice this doubt, suggesting a deeper, more immediate problem that overshadows even such profound philosophical uncertainty.

The second line reveals the immediate and crushing problem: "Jab us ke naaz ko bhi tuin hamil nahin raha" (When I can no longer bear even her pride/coquetry). "Naaz" here refers to the beloved's pride, airs, or coquettishness, which is traditionally a part of the lover-beloved dynamic. A true lover is expected to patiently endure and even cherish the beloved's 'naaz'. The poet's confession that he can no longer "hamil" (bear or tolerate) even this aspect of the beloved signifies a complete erosion of his capacity for love, endurance, or emotional resilience. This inability to tolerate something relatively minor (the beloved's pride) makes his existential doubts seem almost secondary, as if his very capacity to engage with life, even at its most fundamental, has been compromised.

The irony lies in the contrast: how can one ponder the grand question of existence when one's inner strength is so depleted that even a common romantic challenge becomes unbearable? The couplet thus highlights a state of extreme weariness, where personal emotional bankruptcy precedes or even makes redundant grand philosophical inquiries. This isn't a simple demographic question but a rhetorical one, implying that true adherents of faith, in spirit and action, are few and far between. It suggests a critique of hypocrisy or superficial adherence to religious dogma, where outward show might prevail over genuine spirituality.

The second line extends this critique to the realm of religious discourse: "Phir kis beech mein bhi sawaal-e-hasil nahin raha" (Then, in what discussion is there any fruitful question left?). If true faith is scarce, then what is the point of the endless theological debates, sermons, and pronouncements of these religious leaders? Ghalib questions the utility and relevance of such discourse when the fundamental premise – genuine faith among the populace – seems compromised. The "sawaal-e-hasil" (fruitful question) implies that their discussions are empty, leading to no real understanding, spiritual growth, or beneficial outcome, because the groundwork of true belief is lacking.

This couplet showcases Ghalib's characteristic skepticism towards religious orthodoxy and institutionalized faith. He often critiqued the rigidity and formalism of religious leaders, contrasting it with a more personal, nuanced understanding of spirituality. Here, he suggests that the moral and spiritual decay of society renders much of religious debate pointless, as it fails to address the actual state of faith among people.
