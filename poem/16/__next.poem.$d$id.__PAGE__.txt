This suggests that the perceived limitations or difficulties of life are not due to the path itself being `tang` (narrow or constricted). The poet is essentially calling for an awakening of passion and keenness of perception. True love, in its profound and often demanding form as depicted in classical poetry, requires immense strength, devotion, and resilience from the lover, which Ghalib feels his heart lacks.

The second line uses a powerful metaphor to describe this inadequacy: `Jis khaak ko kehte hain ki ye sang nahin` (This dust which they say is not a stone). His heart is likened to `khaak` (dust), suggesting fragility, ephemerality, and lack of substance. While it's implied that his heart is not `sang` (stone-like) – meaning it's not hard, unfeeling, or insensitive – it also doesn't possess the necessary firmness or endurance to bear the intense demands of true love. It's too soft, too easily scattered, perhaps even too vulnerable.

This is a poignant confession of human limitation in the face of an ideal, often divine, love. The heart needs a balance: not as hard as a stone, but not as fleeting as dust. Ghalib's self-assessment reveals a struggle to reconcile his human frailties with the lofty, absolute nature of intense devotion, where suffering and sacrifice are paramount.
