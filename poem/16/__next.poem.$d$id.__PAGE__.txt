Ghalib, using his early pen name 'Asad', posits that the only true remedy for the sorrow of life (*Gham-e-hasti*) is death. This isn't necessarily a call for suicide, but rather a somber reflection on the ultimate release from worldly woes. It suggests a cyclical view of suffering, where life inevitably leads to pain, and death is the final cessation.

The second line employs a classic Ghalibian metaphor: the burning candle. The candle, burning "in every hue" (har rang mein), signifies the varied experiences, emotions, and travails of life, all of which culminate in its eventual extinguishing at dawn. The dawn here symbolizes the end of a cycle, much like death marks the end of life's journey. The candle's burning is a beautiful yet melancholic representation of life's relentless progression towards its inevitable conclusion, a constant state of self-consumption.

The juxtaposition of the existential question with the vivid imagery of the candle highlights Ghalib's mastery of conveying deep philosophical thought through evocative poetic devices. The 'burning' of the candle is not just physical; it metaphorically represents the 'burning' or suffering of human existence. It implies that suffering is intrinsic to life, a fate shared by all living beings, much like a candle's destiny is to burn out.

The poet addresses God, lamenting the state of his eyes, which are not merely shedding tears but "blood-filled tears" (*khoon naab*), indicating an extreme depth of sorrow and pain. This hyperbole is common in classical Urdu poetry to express intense emotional distress. The eyes, brimming with these bloody tears, are described as a "continuous wave" (*silsila mauj ka*). This imagery suggests an unending flow, a torrent of grief that knows no cessation.

The agony is prolonged "until they become a bewildered gaze" (*deedah-e-hairaan hone tak*). A "bewildered gaze" implies eyes that are tired, vacant, and lost in thought due to the intensity and duration of the pain. It's a state of utter exhaustion and despair, where the eyes have shed so many tears that they can no longer perceive clearly, becoming fixed in a look of stunned disbelief and helplessness. The continuous flow of tears transforms into a vacant stare, marking the transition from active weeping to a profound, internalized state of shock and bewilderment.

Ghalib here masterfully conveys the physical and psychological toll of separation. The tears are not just a sign of sadness but a visible manifestation of an internal bleeding, symbolizing a heart that is wounded and continuously hemorrhaging emotion. The journey from flowing blood-tears to a bewildered gaze illustrates the progressive stages of grief, where initial acute pain gives way to a stunned, almost catatonic state.

The second line, "Which I had taken away, lost in spectacle, until it vanished," is intriguing and open to multiple interpretations.
