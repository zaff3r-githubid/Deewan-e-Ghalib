10:T502,This opening couplet sets a profound, melancholic tone, immediately engaging with themes of mortality, transience, and the vastness of the unseen. Ghalib ponders the countless beautiful forms and beings that have existed throughout time. He suggests that only a minuscule fraction of this beauty manifests in the natural world, specifically in the vibrant tulip (lala) and rose (gul). These flowers become symbols of the fleeting beauty that briefly surfaces from the depths of oblivion.

The second line deepens this thought, shifting the focus to the "dust" (khaak), which represents the earth, the grave, and the ultimate destination of all physical forms. Ghalib asks a rhetorical question, marveling at the sheer number and intensity of the beauties that must lie buried and unseen. The implication is that what we perceive as beauty in life and nature is but a fleeting glimpse of an immense, hidden reservoir of countenances and forms that have passed into oblivion.

This couplet is a poignant reflection on the cyclical nature of life and death, the ephemeral existence of beauty, and the humbling realization that most of existence remains hidden from our perception. It's a classic Ghalibian thought – contemplating the grand cosmic scheme through a simple observation.11:T62b,This couplet delves into the intricate psychological states of self-perception, illusion, and confinement. The first line, "Yaan nafas-ha parda-e-auhaam hain ae be-khudi," suggests that our very breaths (nafas), representing life and consciousness, are nothing more than "parda-e-auhaam" (veils of delusions or illusions). This implies that our existence and perceptions are deeply enmeshed in a web of misconceptions and unreality, leading to a state of "be-khudi" (self-oblivion or ecstasy, but here more like a loss of true self-awareness due to illusion).

The second line, "Waan hijaab-e-khud-nigahi se mizah-daan ho gayin," further complicates this. "Hijaab-e-khud-nigahi" means the veil or screen of self-gazing or self-adoration. From this self-absorption, the "mizah-daan" (eyelashes) become a "trap" or "snare." This is a profound metaphor: the very tools of perception (eyelashes protecting the eyes, but also acting as a screen) become a source of confinement when one is engrossed in self-gazing. It suggests that introspection, if it leads to excessive self-absorption or ego, can create its own prison, preventing one from seeing beyond oneself or perceiving reality clearly. The eyelashes, which should open to the world, become a self-imposed barrier.

Together, these lines explore the idea that our internal states—our illusions, our self-absorption, and our loss of genuine self-awareness—are powerful forces that create barriers, preventing us from true liberation or understanding. It's a critique of self-deception and the way the mind can trap itself.12:T5bc,This is a brilliant and deeply symbolic couplet, drawing on the Quranic and biblical story of Prophet Yaqoob (Jacob) and his son Yusuf (Joseph). The first line states that Yaqoob, despite his deep sorrow and separation from Yusuf (who was in a well/prison), did not receive any direct news or "khabar" of his son. This emphasizes the profound distance and lack of information, highlighting Yaqoob's torment and helplessness. He was, in essence, imprisoned by his grief and separation.

The second line delivers the powerful metaphor: "Lekin aankhen rozan-e-deewar-e-zindan ho gayin." His eyes, strained and weeping from constant sorrow and longing, metaphorically became "rozan" (openings or windows) in the "deewar-e-zindan" (wall of the prison). This doesn't mean his physical eyes literally became windows, but rather that his intense longing and constant gaze, despite having nothing to see, pierced through the metaphorical prison of his despair and separation, becoming the only means through which he could "see" or anticipate his son's return. His inward vision, fueled by faith and love, became his only connection to the outside world, a hope that transcends physical barriers.

This couplet beautifully illustrates the power of hope, faith, and profound yearning to transcend physical and emotional confinement. Yaqoob's eyes, even in blindness and sorrow, symbolize an unwavering hope that creates an opening in the seemingly impenetrable walls of despair.13:T5f8,This couplet returns to the theme of personal relationships, but with an added layer of betrayal and misunderstanding. Ghalib laments that even those who were once close to him ("Woh bhi ab") now view him with "adawat ke gumaan" (suspicions of enmity). This is a painful realization, suggesting that his actions, circumstances, or perhaps his very nature have alienated even his former well-wishers, leading them to believe he harbors ill-will. The poet feels misunderstood and unjustly accused.

The second line expands on this irony: "Haan rafeeq-e-shab-e-gham mere jo zindan ho gaye." His "rafeeq-e-shab-e-gham" (companions in the night of sorrow) – those who shared his pain and perhaps offered comfort – have now themselves become his "zindan" (prison). This is a powerful and complex metaphor. It could mean that their misunderstanding and mistrust confine him emotionally, or that their very presence, now tinged with suspicion, feels suffocating and restrictive rather than supportive. It might also imply that their shared sorrow, instead of fostering deeper connection, has somehow transformed into a source of his confinement, perhaps by exacerbating his isolation.

This couplet reflects a profound sense of loneliness and alienation. The poet is not only separated from former confidants but also feels actively misjudged and trapped by the very people who once shared his deepest vulnerabilities. It emphasizes the isolating nature of sorrow and the tragic irony when sources of comfort become sources of pain.14:T7a2,This couplet is a classic example of Ghalib's unique self-awareness and his playful (or sometimes critical) engagement with other poets, specifically Mir Taqi Mir, often considered his predecessor in Urdu ghazal. Ghalib acknowledges Mir's skill ("mashq-e-rafoogari mein to hoshiyar tha Mir") in "rafoogari" (darning or mending). In a literal sense, darning is the art of repairing torn fabric, but here it's a metaphor. Mir was renowned for his ability to express deep, often agonizing, emotions with delicate precision and craftsmanship, meticulously "mending" the brokenness of the heart with his words.

The second line, "Chahiye yeh bhi ke us se rag-e-jaan ho gayin," is where Ghalib offers his own perspective or a challenge. It can be interpreted in a few ways: First, Ghalib might be subtly implying that while Mir excelled at darning (mending superficial tears), Ghalib's poetry aims deeper—to the "rag-e-jaan" (veins of life/soul), meaning he delves into the very essence and raw wounds of existence, perhaps implying that Mir's perfection in darning might not reach the true depth of the soul's suffering or resilience.

Second, it could also mean that Mir's "darning" was so profound that it revealed the very "veins of life" beneath the surface, i.e., his poetry, though meticulously crafted, laid bare the fundamental truths of human experience. Third, it could be self-reflection or aspiration, where Ghalib might be saying that while Mir was a master, Ghalib himself strives to weave the "rag-e-jaan" into his verse, making his poetry not just a repair but an embodiment of life's very pulse. The "chahiye yeh bhi" (this too is required) suggests that Ghalib demands a deeper engagement, a poetry that is not just skillful but vital, pulsating with the very essence of life.
