10:T42f,This profound couplet uses a powerful religious metaphor to express spiritual decline. "Rasm-e-azan" (the ritual of the call to prayer) refers to the mere outward form of religious practice, while "Rooh-e-Bilal" (the spirit of Bilal) evokes the profound devotion, melodious voice, and spiritual purity of Bilal ibn Rabah, the first muezzin of Islam. Ghalib implies that the true spirit and heartfelt devotion (Rooh-e-Bilal) have vanished, leaving behind only an empty ritual.

"Woh namaz aur woh kamal kahan" (Where is that prayer and that perfection now?) further questions the depth and efficacy of current religious practices. The 'kamal' (perfection or excellence) here refers to the spiritual elevation and genuine connection with the divine that true prayer is meant to embody.

This couplet can be interpreted as a commentary on the spiritual state of society or even the poet's personal spiritual journey, suggesting a loss of genuine faith and profound religious experience, replaced by superficial adherence to tradition. It critiques the decline of true piety.11:T421,This couplet further personalizes the theme of lost intensity, likely addressing the beloved or a significant other. The poet suggests that his 'khayal' (thoughts) of the beloved were once as vibrant and profound as the other intense experiences he has been lamenting. The phrase 'ek se' (similar to those) connects this personal longing to the broader sense of loss expressed earlier.

"Ab woh aghosh-e-timsaal kahan?" directly asks about the absence of the 'embrace of images'. 'Timsaal' refers to an image, likeness, or mental picture. 'Aghosh-e-timsaal' implies a deep, immersive mental connection with the beloved's image or memory, where the image itself felt almost tangible and comforting, like an embrace. This could refer to the vividness of memory or the power of imagination to conjure the beloved's presence.

Ghalib mourns the fading of this intense imaginative faculty, where the beloved's image no longer holds the same powerful, embracing quality. The thoughts are there, but the emotional depth and vividness associated with them are gone.12:T449,This poignant couplet introduces a hypothetical scenario where the beloved (or significant figures from the past) might return. The rhetorical question "Jab woh aayenge toh kya dekhenge?" expresses the poet's anxiety and sorrow over the drastic changes that have occurred in their absence. He anticipates their disappointment upon witnessing the current state.

The second line, "Ab toh veeraan hain makaan kahan," provides the stark reality. The 'makaan' (dwellings or homes) are now 'veeraan' (desolate, ruined, empty). This refers not just to physical structures but metaphorically to the inner world of the poet, his heart, his life, or even the places where they once met. The 'kahan' here implies that the vibrant life, the cherished occupants, and the joy that once filled these spaces are utterly gone.

This couplet evokes a sense of irreversible loss and the painful realization that the past cannot be recaptured. It's a lament for a changed reality that even the return of the beloved cannot rectify, as the very essence of what made those places or relationships special has vanished.13:T47b,This concluding couplet summarizes the ghazal's central theme of irreversible loss, turning from specific laments to a broader philosophical acceptance, albeit a sorrowful one. "Bakht-e-bargashata ki shikayat kya" (What complaint is there of reversed fortune?) suggests that complaining about his ill fate or changing luck is pointless. There's an underlying resignation, implying that destiny has played its hand, and it's futile to resist or grumble.

The true source of his sorrow is then revealed in the second line: "Woh zamana woh Dilli woh haal kahan." The poet articulates a profound grief not just for personal misfortune, but for the grander loss of an entire era ('zamana'), his beloved city 'Dilli' (Delhi), and the flourishing 'haal' (condition or state of affairs) that once defined them. This signifies the cultural, political, and social decline witnessed by Ghalib.

This couplet powerfully encapsulates Ghalib's melancholic worldview, where personal suffering is intertwined with the decline of a civilization.
