3:T604,This opening couplet sets the tone for the entire ghazal, establishing Ghalib's characteristic blend of self-pity and defiance. The poet posits the fundamental nature of the heart ("dil") as inherently susceptible to emotion, contrasting it with inanimate objects like "sang-o-khisht" (stone and brick) which are impervious to pain. This rhetorical question, "dard se bhar na aaye kyun" (why shouldn't it be filled with pain?), is both an assertion of the heart's vulnerability and a justification for its suffering.

The second line escalates the emotion, declaring a willingness to cry "hazar baar" (a thousand times). This hyperbolic expression emphasizes the depth of the pain and the heart's capacity for intense sorrow. It's a defiant embrace of suffering, indicating that the poet will not shy away from expressing his agony, no matter how immense.

However, this declaration of grief is immediately followed by another rhetorical question, "koi hamein sataaye kyun" (why should anyone torment me?). This isn't a mere plea for sympathy but a subtle challenge. It acts as a shield, asserting the poet's right to his grief and almost daring anyone to inflict further pain or to question the legitimacy of his suffering. It implies a kind of resignation mixed with a dignified protest against the sources of his pain, be it fate, the beloved, or the world itself, subtly shifting the blame from the sufferer to the tormentor. This complexity of emotion – vulnerability, defiance, and a hint of accusation – is quintessential Ghalib.4:T5e1,This couplet delves into a profound philosophical outlook on life and suffering. Ghalib unequivocally equates "qaid-e-hayat" (the prison of life) with "band-e-gham" (the bondage of sorrow), asserting that these two are fundamentally indistinguishable. Life itself, with all its temporal constraints, worldly attachments, and inherent limitations, is presented as an inescapable form of imprisonment. Within this confinement, sorrow is not merely an occasional visitor but an intrinsic and inseparable companion.

The rhetorical question in the second line, "Maut se pehle aadmi gham se nijaat paaye kyun" (Before death, why should a person ever find liberation from grief?), further reinforces this bleak yet realistic assessment. It suggests that genuine freedom from sorrow is a privilege not granted to mortals within the bounds of life. It implies that true release and peace are only attainable through death, which breaks the existential chains of life and its accompanying pains.

This is a quintessential Ghalibian thought, acknowledging the pervasive nature of grief and the human struggle against it, often concluding that escape is impossible within the confines of life itself. It reflects a stoic acceptance of suffering as an integral and defining part of human existence, challenging conventional notions of finding happiness or complete solace during one's lifetime. The poet implies that to be alive is, by definition, to be in sorrow, making a strong statement about the human condition.5:T72d,This couplet introduces a common ghazal dynamic involving the lover, the beloved, and an external 'other' (often a rival or a moralistic figure). The poet begins by conceding the beloved's perceived flaws—"woh nahin Khuda parast" (they are not God-fearing) and "bewafa sahi" (let them be faithless/disloyal). These attributes are often accusations leveled by society or rivals, attempting to discredit the beloved. However, the lover, Ghalib, accepts these points with a tone of defiant resignation, signaling that such external judgments do not diminish his unwavering devotion.

The phrase "jaao woh bewafa sahi" carries a nuanced meaning. It's not a genuine dismissal of the beloved but a rhetorical flourish. It suggests that even if these accusations are true, they are irrelevant to the lover's deep attachment. It allows Ghalib to demonstrate his profound, unconditional love that transcends conventional morality and external criticisms. The poet essentially tells the accuser to go ahead and label the beloved as such, implying it makes no difference to him.

The second line, "Tum bhi toh mere yaar ko chhudaye kyun," turns the argument directly towards the 'other'—the accuser, rival, or perhaps even God, depending on interpretation. It’s a direct and somewhat indignant challenge: why do *you* interfere and try to separate me from my beloved? The word "chhudaye" (to liberate/release) is used ironically; from the lover's perspective, the beloved is not imprisoned but in a cherished bond, and any attempt to "free" them is an act of unwanted interference and aggression. This dramatic portrayal underscores the profound and often debilitating impact of grief on the human spirit.

Despite this devastating power, the poet poses a rhetorical question: "pe kahan bachein ke dil hai" (but where can one escape, when there is a heart?). This implies a fundamental truth: possessing a heart, the seat of emotions and sensitivity, inherently means being susceptible to sorrow. For a sentient being, escape from pain is not merely difficult but fundamentally impossible. The existence of a heart guarantees the experience of suffering, making it an unavoidable and integral part of human existence.

The second line introduces another layer of complexity, shifting focus to "Ishq-e-junooN pesha" (love, whose profession is madness or passion). The question "isko rawa rakhe kyun" (why should it allow/sanction this?) can be interpreted as the heart questioning its own nature: why does it permit or endure such a "mad" and dangerous pursuit as love, especially given the soul-destroying sorrow it brings? It highlights the paradoxical nature of human experience where intense, often irrational love ("junooN pesha") and profound suffering are inextricably linked.
