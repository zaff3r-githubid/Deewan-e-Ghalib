10:T5f6,This opening couplet sets a tone of profound impatience and the immense difficulty of achieving one's desires, especially in love. The poet uses hyperbole to express the futility of a lover's efforts when the beloved remains indifferent or their attention is so hard-won it might as well be impossible within a lifetime. The "sigh" (aah) is a common symbol of a lover's suffering and prayer; Ghalib suggests even this deep lament takes an eternity to have any impact.

The second line metaphorically equates the beloved's long, unruly tresses with a daunting challenge or an endlessly delayed promise, implying that the lover will die of longing before ever seeing their desires fulfilled or the beloved's heart softened. The 'taming of tresses' can also symbolize the beloved's unpredictable nature or the complexity of their affection, which requires immense patience that the lover simply does not possess in their mortal existence.

This couplet immediately establishes Ghalib's signature blend of philosophical depth and romantic despair. It speaks to the human condition of striving for goals that seem perpetually out of reach, highlighting the gap between intense desire and the seemingly infinite time required for its fruition. This ghazal is renowned for its poignant expression of unattainable love and the harsh realities of waiting. It encapsulates the essence of separation (firaaq) and the agony of delayed gratification. Ghalib here questions the very possibility of survival amidst such protracted suffering.11:T5b7,This couplet uses a powerful metaphor from nature to illustrate the arduous journey towards perfection or fulfillment. The first line presents a vivid image of danger: every single wave of the ocean is not just water but a trap, containing the hundred-toothed jaws of a crocodile, symbolizing countless perils and obstacles. This portrays the world as a treacherous place where danger lurks everywhere, even in seemingly ordinary phenomena.

The second line then applies this imagery to the transformation of a humble drop of water into a precious pearl. The "drop" (qatra) represents the aspiring individual, or perhaps the lover's hope, which must undergo immense pressure and survive countless dangers within the ocean (the world/life/love) to achieve its ultimate, valuable form as a "pearl" (guhar). The poet implies that this transformation is not easy; it's a painful, perilous process, and one must wonder what hardships the drop endures.

This couplet extends the theme of immense patience and struggle from the first couplet, but adds a dimension of transformation and the high cost of achieving something valuable. It suggests that greatness or ultimate desire comes only through surviving extreme trials and tribulations, and that the path is fraught with hidden dangers, much like the ocean's depths conceal predators. It encourages reflection on the sacrifices inherent in the pursuit of excellence or the enduring of suffering for a higher purpose.12:T619,This couplet shifts the focus from universal suffering to a direct address to the beloved, challenging their perceived indifference and cruelty. The lover declares that their "complaint of fidelity" (shikwa-e wafa) – the suffering endured due to their faithfulness – is not something they reserve for the Day of Judgment (mehshar) to be revealed to God. This implies an immediate and earthly need for recognition and justice from the beloved themselves. The use of "O cruel one" (ae zaalim) is a classic address in ghazal poetry, highlighting the beloved's perceived tyranny.

The second line expresses a poignant plea for communication and acknowledgment. The lover asserts their agency ("We too have a tongue in our mouth"), implying they are not merely passive sufferers but capable of articulation and explanation. The phrase "if only you'd ask what our intention is" (kaash poocho ke muddaa kya hai) reveals a deep longing for the beloved to engage, to inquire, to understand the lover's true feelings and suffering, rather than remaining oblivious or dismissive.

This couplet brilliantly combines an accusation of cruelty with a desperate desire for dialogue. It underscores the pain of being misunderstood or entirely ignored by the person whose attention is most craved. Ghalib here captures the lover's frustration with the beloved's aloofness and the yearning for even a simple question that could open the door to expressing a lifetime of unspoken love and loyalty. It is a powerful assertion of the lover's humanity and their right to be heard.13:T5ff,This couplet expresses a profound sense of fatalism and the crushing weight of delayed gratification. The lover, with a bitter irony, acknowledges the possibility that the beloved might eventually cease their indifference ("We concede that you won't neglect us eventually"). However, this concession is immediately undermined by the harsh reality that such a change will come too late. The second line delivers the punch: "We will turn to dust by the time you become aware." This vivid image of becoming "dust" (khaak) signifies death and complete annihilation.

The central tension of this couplet lies in the contrast between the beloved's eventual (but slow) recognition and the lover's mortal lifespan. It suggests that even if the beloved's heart eventually softens or they realize the lover's devotion, the lover will already be long gone, having perished from the agony of waiting and neglect. It's a poignant lament over the unbridgeable gap between the beloved's timeline and the lover's limited existence.

This couplet encapsulates Ghalib's mastery of expressing existential despair within the context of romantic love. It highlights the tragedy of unfulfilled desires and the futility of patience when the desired outcome arrives only after one has ceased to exist. It speaks to the universal human experience of longing for something that might only materialize when it's too late to enjoy it. The resignation in the first line, followed by the stark pronouncement in the second, makes this couplet particularly impactful.14:T4b8,This couplet expresses the lover's anguish over missed opportunities and the beloved's prolonged absence or delay in meeting. The burning heart signifies intense pain and regret, suggesting an internal torment that consumes the lover. The poet laments why they couldn't be together, questioning fate or divine will ("Ya Rab") for this cruel separation and the lack of a shared destiny.

The second line describes the situation as a "spectacle of prolonged delay" (tamasha-e der hone tak). This implies that the entire scenario of their separation and the resulting suffering is a drawn-out, agonizing performance, perhaps even for a divine audience. It speaks to the frustration of love's potential remaining unfulfilled due to an extended period of waiting or circumstances beyond control, making the lover feel like an unwilling participant in a tragic play.

This couplet reinforces the ghazal's overarching theme of the agony of waiting and unfulfilled desire. It suggests a resignation to being overlooked or undervalued.

However, the second line delivers a sharp, ironic twist: "But if he comes for free, what's wrong with that?" This turns the initial self-deprecation on its head. It implies that even if Ghalib (the poet, his work, or his very being) is considered worthless by some, there's no harm in accepting him if there's no cost involved. This line can be read as a playful challenge to his critics, a comment on the transactional nature of value, or a deeper philosophical musing about the inherent worth of things, independent of market value or societal approval.

This couplet showcases Ghalib's unique blend of intellectual playfulness and a profound understanding of human perception and value. It serves as a witty commentary on his own status as a poet, his struggles for recognition, and perhaps a subtle critique of those who might undervalue true genius.
