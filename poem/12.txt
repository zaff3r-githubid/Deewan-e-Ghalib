The poet personifies the "mirror of the garden's worth" (آئینۂ قدرِ گلشن), implying that the true measure or beauty of the garden is now reflected or understood through the poet's own being. This is not a literal mirror, but a metaphorical one that gauges value and beauty. The garden, a traditional symbol of beauty, nature, and sometimes the world itself, now gazes upon the poet, finding its own essence or standard in him.

The second line, "Ek 'ālam hai ki sargarm-e-tamāsha hai mirā" (A whole world is busy in witnessing my spectacle), amplifies this self-referential grandeur. The poet asserts that his very existence, his being, or perhaps his internal turmoil and unique perspective, has become a spectacle for the entire world. This isn't merely boastfulness; it speaks to the depth of his experience and the universal resonance of his emotions and insights. Ghalib often positions himself as an observer and an observed, grappling with existential questions and the nature of reality.

The couplet subtly suggests a reversal of traditional roles. Instead of the poet admiring the garden's beauty, the garden (and by extension, the world) is now contemplating the poet, finding something extraordinary and captivating in him.
