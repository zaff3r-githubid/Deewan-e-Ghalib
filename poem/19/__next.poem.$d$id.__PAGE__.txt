The phrase "Jahan-e-dagar-gun" vividly paints a picture of a world turned topsy-turvy, chaotic, and beyond human control. This often reflects Ghalib's own experiences during turbulent times, such as the decline of the Mughal Empire and the aftermath of the 1857 uprising, though the sentiment is timeless. The initial "kya kijiye" (what can one do?) establishes the central theme of resignation and the futility of action in the face of overwhelming circumstances.

The second line, "Dil-e-bedard ko kya kijiye," introduces a profound internal conflict. "Bedard" literally means 'without pain,' but in this context, it often implies a heart that is numb, insensitive, or perhaps even hardened by continuous suffering, thus unable to fully respond or feel the impact of the external chaos. This could be interpreted in several ways: perhaps the poet's heart has become so accustomed to turmoil that it no longer registers pain, leading to a spiritual or emotional void; or it could imply a criticism of a heart that remains untouched by the widespread distress. This internal dilemma mirrors the external disarray, suggesting that the problem isn't just external but also deeply personal and existential.

Ghalib masterfully uses the rhetorical question "kya kijiye" not to seek an answer, but to underscore the utter lack of options and the deep sense of powerlessness. It’s a lament, a sigh of despair that permeates the entire ghazal. The repetition of the phrase as the radif (refrain) reinforces this feeling of being trapped and directionless, making the couplet resonate with a universal human experience of confronting an unmanageable world and an unresponsive self. The phrase "Jahan-e-dagar-gun" (world turned upside down) can be seen as a direct reflection of this historical context, where traditional structures collapsed, and an old way of life was irrevocably altered. His poetry frequently expresses a deep melancholy and a sense of loss for a bygone era, coupled with a philosophical outlook that questioned the very nature of existence and divine justice.

Beyond the historical context, the ghazal taps into universal human anxieties. Ghalib, as a poet-philosopher, often explored themes of fate, free will, suffering, and the human condition. The internal struggle described in "dil-e-bedard" points to a broader existential crisis where an individual feels disconnected or desensitized amidst widespread distress. The second line shifts to a more concrete, yet equally crushing, concern: "Gham-e-rozgar" – the sorrow of daily life, livelihood, or the struggles of existence. After attributing a state of "vahshat" to fate, Ghalib returns to the central lament, "kya kijiye," emphasizing the inability to overcome these pervasive sorrows. It's a poignant reflection on how existential despair (vahshat) and practical worries (gham-e-rozgar) combine to create an inescapable predicament.

The couplet thus highlights a dual burden: the cosmic indifference of fate shaping one's inner state, and the relentless pressure of worldly responsibilities. Both lead to the same conclusion of helplessness. Ghalib suggests that even if one tries to escape the broader cosmic suffering, the immediate, tangible worries of earning a living and enduring daily struggles remain, equally insurmountable.
