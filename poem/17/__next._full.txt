For the lover, the agony of separation from the beloved is so intense and prolonged that death, in its finality, becomes just another ephemeral experience, perhaps even a lesser one. The phrase "guzri" (passed) emphasizes the transient nature of both death and the night of separation. The lover implies that the suffering of separation is so profound that even death's pain is familiar and, therefore, bearable. It's a testament to the idea that love's torment surpasses even the ultimate physical end.

The second line, "yani is dard mein aaram bhi tha, ye bhi na sahi," adds a layer of ironic acceptance. It suggests that perhaps there was even a form of peace or relief in the pain of death, as it ended the prolonged torment of life without the beloved. However, if even that meager solace is denied or untrue, then "so be it." This again showcases the lover's ultimate resignation and readiness to accept any fate, no matter how devoid of comfort.11:T458,This couplet delves into the blurring of perception caused by profound sorrow and separation. The lover is so immersed in the "shaam-e-firaq" (evening of separation) that the very distinction between light and darkness, dawn and dusk, becomes irrelevant or indistinguishable. The entire world appears shrouded in the gloom of separation. The question "Teergi hai ke hai noor-e-sehar o shaam-e-firaq?" is rhetorical, emphasizing that for the lover, all light, even the dawn's promise, is tainted by the perpetual darkness of longing.

In the second line, the lover dismisses the "noor-e-khursheed" (light of the sun) itself. The sun's light, normally a source of life and clarity, is deemed insignificant if it doesn't align with the emotional state of "shaam-e-ghariban" (the evening of the lonely/stranger/destitute). "Shaam-e-ghariban" traditionally refers to the evening after the battle of Karbala, symbolizing utmost sorrow and desolation. The lover prefers a state of complete desolation that mirrors their inner anguish, making even the sun's light unwelcome if it doesn't conform to this profound sorrow.12:T47f,This couplet presents a complex interplay of the beloved's destructive power and the lover's self-immolation. "Shola-nawa" (eloquent flame) again refers to the beloved, whose captivating nature is likened to a fire that can set hearts ablaze. The first line questions who possesses the power to ignite such a flame, implying the beloved's unique and formidable charm.

The second line, "Kaun jalta hai," is a rhetorical question that suggests the beloved is not the one who burns; it is always the lover who suffers the torment of love. This highlights the inherent imbalance in the relationship: the beloved, though the source of passion, remains untouched by its destructive heat, while the lover is consumed. The phrase "ye andaaz-e-wafa" (this manner/style of loyalty) refers to the lover's constant suffering as a form of devotion. The lover questions this very mode of loyalty—this perpetual state of being burnt and consumed—and yet, in the familiar resignation of "ye bhi na sahi," accepts it as their fate if that's what love demands.
