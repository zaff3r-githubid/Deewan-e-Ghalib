10:T4c8,This opening couplet is a profound philosophical statement on existence, non-existence, and the omnipresence of God. Ghalib begins by asserting that God's existence is independent of creation. Even before anything came into being, God existed, and even if nothing were to exist, God's being would remain untouched. This establishes God as the ultimate, eternal reality, beyond the confines of time and space, and prior to all manifestation.

The second line introduces a paradox of self-awareness and the burden of existence. Ghalib laments that his 'being' or 'existence' (hone) has "drowned" him, implying a sense of entrapment, suffering, or limitation that comes with individuality. He wonders about the state of affairs if he, as an individual entity, had never come into existence. This isn't merely a wish for non-existence, but a deep philosophical inquiry into the nature of self, the illusory nature of individuality, and perhaps a longing to dissolve back into the ultimate reality (God) from which he believes he originated.

It touches upon Sufi concepts of 'fana' (annihilation of self) and 'baqa' (subsistence in God), suggesting a profound weariness with the burdens of ego and the desire for ultimate unity.11:T467,This couplet speaks to the extreme depths of despair and emotional numbness Ghalib often portrays. The poet declares that his sorrow has rendered him so insensitive (be-ḥiss) that the prospect of his head being severed holds no further pain or concern. This hyperbole emphasizes the overwhelming nature of his internal suffering, suggesting that physical pain or death pales in comparison to the emotional agony he endures. The imagery is stark and powerful, conveying a complete detachment from bodily harm.

The second line further intensifies this sentiment with dark humor and resignation. He imagines that if his head were not separated from his body (presumably by execution or extreme violence), it would simply be resting on his lap (zānū par dharā hotā). This suggests a state of utter exhaustion, surrender, or perhaps a macabre sense of peace in the face of absolute suffering. The head, usually a symbol of thought and identity, becomes a mere object, heavy and without purpose, resting aimlessly. It highlights the poet's profound weariness with life's burdens and his indifference to its ultimate cessation.12:T49c,This couplet addresses the resilience, or perhaps the resignation, of life. Ghalib asserts that hope and aspiration are not solely dependent on the prospect of death or an afterlife. This challenges conventional religious or philosophical views that often link ultimate hope to salvation or liberation through death. Instead, Ghalib suggests a more immanent view, where life itself, with all its struggles, carries its own meaning and momentum. He implies that existence is not merely a waiting period for something beyond.

The second line conveys a sense of passive endurance. "We are alive, so we will keep living; what else could happen?" This isn't a joyful affirmation of life but a statement of its inherent persistence. It reflects a stoic acceptance of one's fate – as long as there is breath, there is life, and one must continue to navigate it. The phrase "kyā hotā" (what else could happen?) underscores a sense of inevitability and a lack of alternative. It could be interpreted as a weary acceptance of the ongoing struggle, or perhaps a subtle defiance, suggesting that even in the absence of grand hopes tied to death, life simply continues, demanding its due.13:T4fb,This couplet delves into the human motivation for continued engagement with life, even amidst its difficulties. Ghalib suggests that it is "some worldly pleasure" (duniyā kī lazzat) that keeps humanity, or specifically himself, tethered to the material world. This isn't necessarily a grand joy, but perhaps the small, fleeting delights, attachments, or distractions that prevent complete disillusionment and withdrawal. This 'lazzat' acts as a subtle hook, a reason, however minor, to continue living and interacting with the world.

The second line offers a stark counterfactual: "otherwise, we would think that the heart, too, was an object of senselessness (be-dimāġhī kā dharā hotā)." 'Be-dimāġhī' literally means 'brainlessness' or 'folly', here implying something devoid of purpose, logic, or meaning. The heart, traditionally the seat of emotions, love, and life itself, would be perceived as a mere senseless object if not for these worldly attractions. This reflects Ghalib's often skeptical view of human passions and the inherent meaninglessness he sometimes attributes to existence, only to be mitigated by the small lures of the material world. It suggests that without these distractions, the core of human experience would feel empty and illogical.14:T583,This couplet is a profound and paradoxical meditation on the nature of the relationship between humanity (or the poet) and the Divine (referred to as 'tujh se' - from/with You). Ghalib expresses a contradictory state: "There is no relation with You, yet there is some relation." This captures the ineffable and often illogical nature of faith and spiritual connection. On one hand, the absolute transcendence of God suggests a complete separation from the finite human experience. On the other hand, the very act of questioning, seeking, or acknowledging God implies a connection, however subtle or undefined. It speaks to the mystery of divine-human interaction, which defies simple classification.

The second line further elaborates on this paradox through a series of rhetorical questions that probe the existence and nature of this relationship. "If there is (a relation), then why is it?" This questions the rationale or purpose behind such a connection, perhaps reflecting on the suffering or lack of clear divine intervention in the world. "If there is not, then what would happen?" This implies that the absence of such a connection would lead to an even greater void, suggesting that even an unexplainable or difficult relationship with the Divine is preferable to complete detachment. This is a common trope in ghazal poetry where the beloved's attention to a rival causes pain to the lover. The second line is Ghalib's sharp, rhetorical comeback: "You tell me, why wouldn't the heart have that connection (to be troubled)?" It's an affirmation of the natural human emotional response to love, attachment, and perceived competition. The second line echoes this sentiment, but for love: "Love, though it snatches life (jāñ-sitāñ)," is also inescapable ("pe kahāñ bacheñ"), again, "because it is the heart." Here, Ghalib posits love not as a blissful emotion but as a powerful, potentially destructive force that claims one's very being. Both sorrow and love are presented as inherent conditions of the human heart, suggesting that to be human, to possess a heart, is to be vulnerable to these profound and often painful experiences.
