3:T78d,This opening couplet by Ghalib encapsulates a profound sense of irony and unfulfilled desire. The poet expresses regret, not for his own suffering in love, but for the beloved's eye (chashm-e-dildar) not having faced "disgrace" or public recognition of the lover's intense devotion. He yearns for his relentless waiting and agony to have been so profound that it would have caused some visible impact or embarrassment to the beloved, forcing an acknowledgement of his plight.

The second line, "Chashmi hi na nikli dar intizar," further deepens this paradoxical lament. "Chashmi" here refers to the lover's own eye. The phrase suggests that despite the excruciatingly long and painful wait for a glimpse of the beloved, his eye did not even 'emerge' or pop out of its socket due to the strain. This can be interpreted as a failure of his suffering to manifest dramatically enough to create a spectacle, or to prompt any reaction from the beloved.

The poet's desire for the beloved's "ruswai" (disgrace) is not malicious, but a desperate cry for recognition. He wishes his pain were so immense and public that it would leave no option for the beloved but to acknowledge it, even if through shame. The unrequited nature of his love is highlighted by the fact that even his extreme suffering remained unnoticed and had no tangible effect on the object of his affection, failing even to cause a slight stir or a moment of self-consciousness in the beloved.

This couplet is a classic example of Ghalib's philosophical depth, where he transcends conventional expressions of love and sorrow. Instead of simply lamenting his fate, he intellectualizes his suffering, pointing out the irony that his very pain wasn't impactful enough to 'disgrace' the beloved's indifference or cause his own eye to burst forth from anticipation.
