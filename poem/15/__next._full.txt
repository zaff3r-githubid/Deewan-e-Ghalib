10:T676,This opening couplet immediately sets a tone of wistful longing and past glory. The poet laments the passage of a significant amount of time since he last had the joy of hosting his "yaar" (beloved or friend). This 'yaar' could be a literal beloved, a spiritual guide, or even a metaphorical representation of joy, inspiration, or divine presence. The act of hosting implies intimacy, shared space, and mutual delight. The phrase "mehman kiye hue" suggests a cherished memory of a rare and precious occasion.

The second line introduces a vibrant image: "Josh-e-qadah se bazm charaghan kiye hue" – illuminating the gathering with the fervor of the wine-cup. This isn't merely about physical light but a metaphorical illumination derived from intense passion, perhaps the intoxicating effect of love, poetry, or spiritual ecstasy. The "bazm" (gathering) could be an actual assembly of friends, or it could represent the poet's inner world, his heart, which was once alight with the 'josh' (passion/fervor) of this special connection. The past tense of "kiye hue" reinforces that this joyous, illuminated state is now a memory, contributing to the sense of longing.

The couplet masterfully contrasts the present state of absence with the vibrant memory of presence. The 'yaar' and the 'josh-e-qadah' are intertwined, suggesting that the beloved's presence was the source of this profound passion and light. The absence of the beloved means both the guest and the accompanying revelry/illumination are no longer present, leaving a void that the poet deeply feels. It's a poignant opening, instantly drawing the reader into the poet's emotional landscape.11:T68b,Following the lament of the beloved's absence, this couplet introduces a new flicker of hope or perhaps renewed agitation. The phrase "Baaz ik bheer si padne lagi hai dil mein" depicts a crowd gathering in the heart. This "bheer" (crowd) is not a welcome, organized assembly but rather an unbidden, somewhat chaotic throng. It could represent a resurgence of emotions, desires, anxieties, or memories associated with the beloved, stirring up the poet's inner world after a period of quietude. The "si" (like) suggests an approximation, a feeling akin to a crowd, hinting at an internal commotion rather than a literal one.

The second line, "Hai isi koocha-o-bazaar mein samaan kiye hue," places the beloved within the familiar, mundane world of streets and markets, making preparations. This contrast is striking: the poet's heart is in turmoil, anticipating, while the beloved is engaged in seemingly ordinary acts. The 'samaan' (preparations) could imply an impending visit, a journey, or simply the beloved's life continuing, oblivious to or unconcerned with the poet's inner state. It might also suggest that the 'preparations' are for the beloved's own life or destiny, which may or may not involve the poet.

The tension lies between the internal chaos of the lover and the external, perhaps indifferent, activity of the beloved. The "same street and market" suggests proximity yet distance, a shared environment where one waits in emotional upheaval while the other goes about their business. This couplet captures the agonizing hope and despair of unrequited or delayed love, where every slight indication from the beloved fuels immense internal churn for the lover.12:T682,This couplet delves into the intense and relentless nature of the lover's desire to see the beloved. The "garmi-e-shauq-e-nazaara" – the intense heat or fervor of the longing to gaze upon the beloved – is so overwhelming that it "na bakhshi mohlat," meaning it did not grant any respite or moment of peace. This desire is portrayed as an unceasing, burning force that consumes the lover, leaving no room for tranquility or distraction. It highlights the all-encompassing nature of this longing, where the very act of seeking a glimpse becomes an internal torment.

The second line presents a powerful, almost surreal image: "Sad rah aaina-khana ko hairan kiye hue" – leaving a hundred mirror-houses bewildered in amazement. This is a characteristic Ghalibian hyperbole. A 'mirror-house' (aaina-khana) is a place designed to reflect, to multiply images, and by extension, to capture beauty. The 'hundred paths' (sad rah) could imply countless attempts to find a reflection or countless places where the beloved's image might be seen. Yet, this intense longing of the poet, this burning desire to merely 'behold', leaves even the mirrors, which are supposed to reflect, utterly astonished and bewildered.

The bewilderment of the mirror-houses suggests that the beloved's beauty or the intensity of the lover's gaze is so profound that it transcends even the capacity of mirrors to fully capture or comprehend it. It could also imply that the lover's own reflection, distorted by the intensity of longing, is what bewilders the mirrors. The couplet thus magnifies both the beloved's extraordinary essence and the lover's overwhelming, almost unbearable, passion.13:T6aa,This couplet poignantly describes the public nature of a private affair, and the beloved's paradoxical influence. "Koo ba koo phail gai baat shanaasaai ki" indicates that the news of the poet's acquaintance or relationship with the beloved has spread everywhere, from street to street. This suggests that their connection, no matter how discreet they tried to be, has become common gossip or knowledge. This public exposure is often a source of torment for the lover in ghazal tradition, as it implies a loss of privacy and often societal judgment.

The second line presents a profound irony and a hallmark of Ghalib's wit: "Us ne parde mein bhi rakha mujhe uriyaan kiye hue." The beloved, who herself lives 'in parda' (behind a veil, in seclusion, maintaining modesty and secrecy), has nonetheless left the poet 'uriyaan' (naked, exposed, bare). This is a brilliant juxtaposition. While the beloved might be physically veiled or socially secretive, her impact on the poet is such that he is stripped bare – emotionally, reputationally, and perhaps even spiritually. His vulnerability and devotion are laid open for all to see, precisely because of his association with her.

The couplet highlights the power dynamic inherent in love, where the beloved, even in her guarded state, can inadvertently or intentionally expose the lover. It speaks to the idea that love, especially for a captivating and elusive beloved, leaves the lover utterly defenceless and transparent to the world, regardless of how much the beloved maintains her own privacy. The contrast between the beloved's 'veil' and the lover's 'nakedness' is a masterstroke, capturing the essence of being consumed by love and its consequences.14:T68b,This couplet is an exquisite tribute to the beloved's profound impact, using highly metaphorical language. "Chashm-e-bad door" is an idiomatic phrase, meaning "may the evil eye be far away," a common invocation to ward off jealousy or misfortune from something precious. This immediately elevates the beloved to a status of immense value and vulnerability, emphasizing her preciousness. The beloved is then described as "woh ganjina-e-ma'ani," a 'treasure of meaning.' This suggests that her very being, her presence, her words, and her essence are not merely beautiful but are imbued with deep, profound significance and insight, a source of endless wisdom or truth.

The effect of this 'treasure of meaning' is described in the second line: "Dil ke khamyaze ko khandaan kiye hue" – that she makes even the yawning of the heart smile. A 'khamyaza' (yawn) is typically associated with weariness, boredom, fatigue, or a state of despondency. To say that the beloved makes even this yawning heart 'khandaan' (smiling, laughing) is a powerful metaphor. It implies that her presence or even her thought is so invigorating, so joyous, and so deeply satisfying that it transforms weariness into cheerfulness, sorrow into joy, and existential fatigue into a vibrant smile. It's a profound transformation from languor to liveliness.

This couplet portrays the beloved not just as a source of romantic love, but as an existential comfort and intellectual/spiritual stimulus. She brings light and meaning to the deepest, most tired corners of the lover's being, turning despair into delight. "Phir wuhi shokhi-o-rindi hai hamare bas mein" suggests a resurgence of his characteristic playfulness, defiance, and unconventionality (rindi). 'Rindi' often implies a joyous disregard for social norms, a spirit of bohemianism or even spiritual rebellion, often associated with a carefree, wine-loving disposition. The phrase "hamare bas mein" indicates that this state of mischievous revelry is something he actively embraces or finds himself capable of once more, perhaps after a period of somberness.

However, this reassertion of "shokhi-o-rindi" is immediately tempered and contextualized by the second line: "Phir wuhi dil ko jigar tishna-e-sozan kiye hue." This means that along with the mischief and revelry, there is a renewed sense of burning thirst in the heart and liver (jigar). The liver, in Urdu poetry, is often associated with intense emotions, courage, and suffering. "Tishna-e-sozan" signifies a burning thirst, an unquenchable longing that causes inner torment. Thus, the couplet presents a paradox: the return to outward revelry and playfulness coexists with, and perhaps even stems from, a deep, burning, and unfulfilled longing within.

The couplet vividly illustrates the complex inner world of the Ghalibian lover, who often masks profound suffering with wit and defiance. The "shokhi-o-rindi" might be a coping mechanism, a way to navigate the persistent "tishna-e-sozan" of the heart. It's a "good thought" not because it's necessarily true, but because it provides solace and distraction.

This couplet showcases Ghalib's unique blend of intellectual honesty and human empathy. He doesn't outright reject faith but subtly critiques its conventional interpretations, while simultaneously recognizing its vital role in providing comfort and hope to the human psyche.
