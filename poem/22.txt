10:T579,Ghalib opens this ghazal with a powerful declaration of his desire for complete isolation. He expresses a longing to move to a place utterly devoid of human presence, where the very concept of 'company' ceases to exist. This isn't just a wish for privacy, but a profound yearning for an existence untouched by the complexities, expectations, and often the disappointments of human interaction.

The second line intensifies this desire, specifying the nature of the desired solitude. Not only should there be no one, but specifically, no one with whom to engage in conversation ("ham-sukhan") or even to share a common language ("ham-zabaan"). This suggests a rejection of all forms of communication and understanding, implying that even shared language, which typically fosters connection, has become a source of burden or misunderstanding for the poet. It's a quest for an unburdened self, free from the need to explain, justify, or even simply interact.

This couplet sets the melancholic and deeply introspective tone for the entire ghazal, establishing a theme of radical withdrawal. It speaks to a universal human experience of feeling overwhelmed and seeking an ultimate escape from societal pressures and the often-painful intricacies of human relationships. Ghalib, known for his philosophical depth, transforms a simple wish for loneliness into a profound statement about existential fatigue.11:T688,Continuing the theme of ultimate isolation, Ghalib now imagines the physical manifestation of his desired solitude. The idea of a "house without doors or walls" is a powerful paradox. A house traditionally provides shelter, privacy, and defines boundaries, but Ghalib's desired abode negates these very functions. This imagery suggests a complete rejection of conventional shelter and the social structures it represents. It implies a state of vulnerability that paradoxically brings a sense of freedom from intrusion and societal expectations. Without doors, there's no entry or exit in the conventional sense, symbolizing a break from the outside world. Without walls, there's no boundary, signifying an ultimate openness to the void and an absence of confining structures.

The second line extends this desire for unadulterated solitude. The absence of a "hamsaaya" (neighbor) ensures no social interaction, no casual greetings, no shared existence. More intriguingly, the absence of a "paasbaan" (guard or protector) signifies a rejection of protection and surveillance, both external and internal. It's a desire to be beyond supervision, beyond judgment, and to surrender entirely to one's own isolated existence, free from even the benevolent watch of others. This is a radical embrace of self-sufficiency, even at the cost of vulnerability.

This couplet paints a vivid picture of a world utterly devoid of human connection and societal constructs, emphasizing the poet's profound longing to transcend all conventional forms of dwelling and social engagement. It's a poetic construction of an anti-social utopia, where true freedom is found in absolute nothingness.12:T59f,This couplet pushes the desire for isolation to its most extreme and poignant conclusion, contemplating the ultimate moments of human existence: illness and death. The poet wishes for complete autonomy even in his most vulnerable state. If he falls ill, he desires no "teemaar-daar" (nurse or caregiver). This is a rejection of comfort, empathy, and the fundamental human need for care during distress. It underscores a profound weariness with human connection, implying that even acts of kindness and support have become unbearable, perhaps due to the burden of reciprocal obligation or the perceived insincerity of such gestures.

The second line takes this even further into the realm of mortality. Ghalib expresses a wish that if he were to die, there should be "no nauha-khwaan" (no one to lament or mourn). This is a wish for a death completely unmarked by human emotion, remembrance, or ritual. It signifies a desire to depart from the world without leaving any trace, without causing any sorrow, and without being remembered. It's the ultimate erasure of one's existence from the collective human memory.

This couplet reveals the depth of the poet's disillusionment, suggesting that even the most fundamental human bonds and rituals surrounding life and death have lost their meaning or have become undesirable. It's a powerful statement on existential loneliness and the ultimate longing for detachment from all worldly concerns.13:T73b,This couplet offers a poignant insight into the internal state that drives the poet's desire for extreme isolation. Ghalib describes an internal paradox: the "fire of my heart is dying out, moment by moment," yet simultaneously, "I burn." This imagery suggests a slow, agonizing process of emotional exhaustion and spiritual decay. The "fire" could represent passion, hope, love, or the very essence of life force, which is dwindling. However, the continuous 'burning' implies a lingering pain, a slow internal incineration, perhaps from past wounds or the very process of detachment. It's a state of profound weariness, where the capacity for joy and sorrow is diminishing, yet the torment persists.

The second line reiterates and deepens the plea for absolute solitude, focusing specifically on emotional ties. "Let no one be mine" expresses a desire to sever all bonds of ownership, belonging, and attachment. This isn't just about not having companions, but about eradicating the very notion of personal connection – no one to claim, and no one to be claimed by. This is immediately followed by "and let there be no compassionate one." This is perhaps the most heartbreaking demand, as compassion is often considered a balm for suffering. Ghalib's rejection of a "mehrbaan" (compassionate one) implies that even pity or kindness would be an intrusion, a reminder of a connection he desperately wishes to escape, or perhaps that he feels undeserving of, or simply too broken to receive. It reinforces the desire for complete self-reliance in suffering, free from the obligations and discomforts that even well-meaning sympathy can bring.

This couplet portrays a soul at its nadir, grappling with internal anguish that finds expression in a radical rejection of human comfort and connection, seeking solace in an unshared and unpitied existence.14:T627,In the concluding couplet (maqta), Ghalib employs a brilliant poetic device, referring to himself in the third person. The poet, as an observer, recounts stopping 'Ghalib' (his poetic persona) on his way to an unspecified destination. This self-referential observation creates a layer of detachment, allowing the poet to reflect on his own profound desire for isolation as if it were an external phenomenon. It's a clever way to present the extremity of his internal state, viewing his own inclination for solitude with an almost clinical or resigned perspective. The "place" is deliberately left vague, emphasizing that the desire for isolation is not tied to a specific location but to an internal state of being.

The second line delivers the punchline, revealing the ultimate intention of the persona: "If he were to go, he would say that there should be no one here." This confirms the ghazal's central theme of absolute emptiness and solitude. The 'here' in this context implies the destination he was headed, but also, by extension, any place he might inhabit. The final statement encapsulates the entire ghazal's philosophy: a world utterly devoid of human presence, interactions, and connections.

This maqta serves as a powerful summary, solidifying the ghazal's message of ultimate withdrawal. By stepping outside himself, Ghalib not only signs off but also provides a meta-commentary on the intensity and unwavering nature of his longing for an existence untouched by others.
